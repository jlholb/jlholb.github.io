**my philosophy**

what keeps us from achieving our health goals? why aren't we able to do the things we rationally know how to do? knowing how to live healthily, being healthy, and keeping our health is tougher than most folks would like to admit.

 the science of behavior change has, and continues to require rigorous, scientific analysis to move forward. In the face of gut feelings and anecdotal evidence, systematic analysis is the way forward.

 **my three health behavior pillars**

 *behavior is the ultimate measure*

while working as a group facilitator at a substance-use treatment center, the essence of struggle was ever present in each patients life. the connecting thread through their lives was seldom "how do i stop drinking", but a relentless belief that they lacked the power to do so. no matter the precision with which cogntiive-behavioral therapy was administered, the robust support system the patient had, or dosage of a pharmaceutical intervention, patients in the core often felt incapable. these folks knew coping mechanisms front and backwards, spent hundreds of hours in group and individual therapy, and had robust plans for their life after treatment. but, with horrifying frequency, patients core beliefs about change were lacking.

this is at the heart of what health education must address, as knowledge is necessary, but rarely sufficient. we can measure a program by how much information it delivers, or we can measure it by whether behavior actually changes, but only the second measure matters. what a person knows, intends, or can recite is important, but we must care about the outcome. 

*we can only change what we can measure*

behavior change is difficult to measure, and it is measured badly more often than we would like to admit. it is tempting to trust the story a program tells about itself, that attitudes shifted, that awareness grew, that people left better off. but a claim is not data built by the scientific process. if we cannot define the behavior we are trying to change, specify who we are trying to reach, and measure whether they actually moved, then we ultimately are guessing.

*we must meet individuals where they are at*

 it is easy to frame the failure to change as a failure of willpower or intelligence. but people have reasons, constraints, and competing priorities that make sense from where they stand, and any program or intervention that ignores those reasons will lose to them every time. this is why understanding the audience must be a priority. the barriers people face, the costs they perceive, and the behaviors already competing for their time are not background details and do play a role in whether interventions will be implemented successfully. treating people as the experts in their own lives is both more respectful and more effective.

 *in summary*

 health education is at its best when it is rigorously structured. behavior is the outcome, measurement is the constraint, and the audience is a substantial stakeholder. the goal is not just to be heard, it is to be effective.
